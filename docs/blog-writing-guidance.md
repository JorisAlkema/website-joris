# Blog writing guidance

- Write for someone arriving from LinkedIn who wants to understand what Joris does. Use plain, direct English and a personal, pragmatic voice.
- Open with a concrete, understandable problem. Explain why it matters before introducing names, terminology, or implementation details.
- Separate the broader purpose from the actual starting point of the project. Base both on the source material and Joris's account; do not invent motivations.
- Show CS understanding through concrete choices: what was built or changed, how it works, and why that choice mattered.
- Credit existing algorithms, implementations, and collaborators accurately. Use brief inline links rather than a related-work introduction.
- Keep claims within the evidence. Distinguish observations from explanations, and future ambitions from demonstrated results.
- Include technical precision when it helps the reader understand the method or result. Omit coordinate conventions, bookkeeping, and academic qualifications that interrupt the story.
- Give every sentence a purpose. Connect thesis or publication mentions to the story; avoid dangling status sentences and repeated framing.
- Keep publication and thesis details visually secondary with smaller, muted text, spacing, and expandable details. The story takes the foreground.
- Provide a useful thesis or repository link, without file-by-file reading directions or implementation-link sentences. Keep verification reports out of the article.
- Use relevant topic tags. Check factual claims, the site build, and desktop/mobile readability. Commit or push when requested.
- Scale length to the importance of the project. The BSc post should be a brief account of roughly 200–300 words, without pseudocode or a literature review.
